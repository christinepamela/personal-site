Fiction vs non-fiction: what you submit

  • Fiction: finish and polish the whole manuscript first. You then send a query letter, usually a 1–2 page synopsis and the opening pages the agent asks for.
  • Non-fiction: you sell it from a book proposal and sample chapters, before the book is finished.
  • Memoir / narrative non-fiction: usually sold with a proposal, but agents often want polished sample chapters because voice matters so much.
  • Scholarly: a proposal form, CV and sample material, followed by external peer review.

Who does what in a trade deal

Author → Agent → Publisher. The agent pitches you and negotiates the contract. The publisher pays an advance, produces and distributes the book, and pays royalties after the advance has been earned back. The agent is paid only when you are paid.

Publishers usually send royalty statements twice a year, normally through the agent, who deducts commission and passes the rest on.

Rights: what you grant, what to negotiate

Who owns what

  • In a standard trade contract you keep the copyright. You grant the publisher an exclusive licence to publish in set formats and territories.
  • Anything not expressly granted is normally reserved to you. Look for a "reserved rights" clause.
  • Be cautious of any clause that assigns your copyright, treats the book as "work made for hire", or grants "all rights throughout the universe".
  • Print, ebook and audio should be separable so you can reclaim a format the publisher is not using.

Subsidiary rights splits

These are rights the publisher licenses to third parties (translation, audio, serial, book club, film). The contract usually lists your share.

  • A 50:50 split is a common opening offer. Skilled negotiators have moved some splits to around 80:20 for foreign rights and 90:10 for first serial.
  • For audio sublicensed to a third-party producer, author shares of roughly 50–75% are reported, and 75–80% for foreign rights when the publisher has no capacity to sell them.
  • If a publisher has never sold foreign rights for comparable titles, ask why they need yours.

Getting your book back (reversion)

  • The old "out of print" test is weak now that books never go out of print. Ask for a clause triggered by low sales or income instead, for example under about 250 copies a year, or $250–$500 of income in a year.
  • Ask that unexercised subsidiary rights revert if unused 18–24 months after publication.
  • Define reversion separately for print, ebook and audio.

Clauses that quietly cost you

  • Option clause on your "next book".
  • Non-compete clauses limiting what else you can publish.
  • Cross-collateralisation: one book's shortfall deducted from another's earnings.
  • Recouping the advance from subsidiary-rights income before you see it.
  • AI-training and data-mining language. The Authors Guild has added AI-specific clauses to its model contract.
  • Approval rights over cover and any abridgement or adaptation.
Earning out: your royalties first repay the advance. Only after that do you receive more money. Many successful books never earn out, and that is not necessarily a bad outcome, but it means the advance is often the main money you will see.

4Agents: who to approach and where to find them

Most trade publishers, especially the largest, rarely read unagented submissions. An agent sells your book, negotiates the contract and is paid a commission only when you are paid.

How to build a shortlist

  1. List 5–10 recent books similar to yours. Check their acknowledgements pages, which usually thank the agent.
  2. Look each agent up on their agency site and on a database below. Confirm they are open to queries and represent your category.
  3. Sort them into tiers: top-tier (strongest fit), mid-tier (good fit) and watchlist (general fit or less active).
  4. Read their submission rules and send exactly what they ask for. Some want queries one agent at a time, and many will not open unrequested attachments.
  5. Record everything in the tracker below, with the date you will follow up.

Where to search for agents

  • QueryTracker: filterable agent database with response data and a submission tracker.
  • Publishers Marketplace: paid; shows which agents are actually making sales, and in which categories.
  • Manuscript Wish List: agents and editors say what they are looking for.
  • Poets & Writers agent database: includes submission guidelines.
  • AAR member directory: members follow a code of ethics, including no reading fees.
  • AgentQuery: free genre-based search. Confirm details are current before relying on them.

7Red flags and trusted resources

Agents

  • Asks for a reading, evaluation, marketing or up-front fee. Reputable agents are paid by commission on your sales.
  • Contacts you out of the blue offering representation.
  • Pushes paid editing or other services from a company they own.
  • Can't name any recent sales or clients.

Hybrid and paid publishers

  • Doesn't meet every IBPA hybrid criterion. IBPA says partial compliance is not enough.
  • Says yes within hours, or accepts almost everything. Real hybrids vet submissions.
  • Hard sales pitches, deadlines and discounts that expire.
  • Charges for marketing, or holds your files or ISBNs hostage.
  • No clear breakdown of where your money goes.

Contracts (any route)

  • Rights for the full life of copyright with no workable reversion.
  • Rights bundled into one block, or a sweeping all-rights grant.
  • Option, non-compete and cross-collateralisation clauses left unexamined.
  • No right of approval over cover or adaptations.

Check before you commit

  • Writer Beware: warnings about fee-charging agents, vanity presses and scams.
  • IBPA Hybrid Publisher Criteria.
  • Authors Guild: hybrid publishing contracts. The Guild also offers contract review to members.
  • AAR code of ethics for agents.
